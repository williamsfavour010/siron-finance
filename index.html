Notes from one of us, for all of us

Let us actually do the math on our money.

Over the last year or two, I have probably spoken to most of you one to one about one investment or another. Stocks, 401(k)s, Roth IRAs, real estate, Nigeria investments, savings accounts, businesses, insurance. Somewhere along the way I realised I had also spent a ridiculous amount of time researching and comparing all of this myself, and talking to people a few years ahead of us. This site is me putting those notes in one place, so we can compare them properly instead of each figuring it out alone.

I am not an advisor and this is not me telling anyone what to do. Is what you are doing with money right now going to get you where you want to be in 10 to 20 years? And if not, what is the first thing you would change?

01 · Start Here

First, the order things should happen in

Can I be honest? Most of the money stress I see in our group is not really an investing problem. It is a sequencing problem. We ask "what should I buy" before we have answered "what am I trying to build, and what needs to be true first". Do these in order, and most of the later decisions get simpler.

  1. Set the goal, in numbersWhat are you actually aiming at, and by when? A house deposit, a move, a business, breathing room. A vague goal produces vague saving. A number and a date produce a monthly amount, and a monthly amount is something you can actually act on.
  2. Build a starter emergency fundOne month of essential spending first, in an account you can reach quickly. This is what stops one bad week from becoming debt.
  3. Kill expensive debtAny debt charging high interest, credit cards especially, is an emergency wearing a normal outfit. Paying it off is a guaranteed return equal to its interest rate, and you will not beat that reliably anywhere else.
  4. Capture the full employer matchIf your employer matches pension or retirement contributions, contribute at least enough to get the whole match. It is part of your pay. Leaving it on the table is volunteering for a pay cut.
  5. Then invest, regularly and boringlyWith the base in place, invest a fixed amount on payday into diversified funds inside the right tax wrapper for your country. Section 4 and the country playbooks cover how. Boring is a feature here, not a flaw.

Know your number

Most of us say we want financial freedom, and almost none of us can say what it costs. That gap is where a lot of the anxiety lives. You cannot tell whether you are on track, behind, or quietly fine, because there is no track. So let us build the number, slowly, with an example. Change every figure to your own as you read.

Step one: what do you actually spend in a year? Not what you earn, and not what you wish you spent. What a comfortable year costs you, needs and reasonable wants included. Say it is $100,000 a year for the life you are picturing. If your real spending is lower, and for many of us starting out it is closer to $40,000, your number will be far smaller, and we will come back to that.

Step two: multiply by the years. Say you are 23 and you plan prudently for living to 100. That is 77 years. Flat, with no inflation, $100,000 times 77 is $7.7 million. Already a big number. Now add inflation at 3% a year, which means each future year costs a little more than the one before. The same 77 years of $100,000 a year, in today's terms, adds up to roughly $29 million of total spending across the lifetime. Sit with that for a second, because the next step is the one that makes it manageable.

Step three: you never need that pile in cash. Money invested does work while you spend it. At a return of about 4% a year above inflation, a pot of roughly $2.4 million can fund $100,000 a year for those 77 years. The shortcut form of the same idea is the 25 times rule: take your yearly spending and multiply by 25. At $100,000 a year that is $2.5 million. At $40,000 a year, which is a realistic, comfortable spend for plenty of people in our group, it is about $1 million. Same formula, very different mountain, and yours depends on your life, not on anyone else's.

Go deeperHow the number changes as life changes, and the retire early case

Your number is not fixed at 23, and pretending it is would be its own kind of dishonesty. It moves when your life moves. A partner, children, supporting parents, a more expensive city, a simpler life than you expected: each one changes the yearly spending line, and the whole calculation follows from that one line. Someone who stays single with low costs has a different number from someone raising four kids and helping family back home, and neither number is more virtuous. Revisit it once a year, the way you revisit the budget, and let it be a current estimate rather than a promise you made to yourself at 23.

The early retirement case is worth seeing once, with real figures. To reach a $2 million pot at 40, starting at 23 and assuming 7% a year, you would need to invest roughly $5,100 a month. That is a serious amount, and naming it is the point. For some people in high paying roles it is possible. For most of us it is not, yet, and knowing the price lets you choose a later date or a smaller pot with your eyes open instead of vaguely feeling behind. And compounding does not stop at 40: left invested at 7% with nothing added and nothing withdrawn, $2 million becomes about $7.7 million by 60. Try your own numbers in the compound calculator in Tools.

Here is why I keep coming back to this exercise. The moment you have a number, the questions change. Instead of "am I doing enough", which has no answer and follows you around, you get "am I making more than my spending needs, and is the surplus invested toward the pot", which does have an answer. You can check it in a monthly close. That is most of what this site is: turning a fog into a few numbers you can actually steer by.

Where the 25 times frame comes from, honestly. It is not a law of nature. It comes from US research. William Bengen's 1994 study found that about 4.15% was the highest starting withdrawal rate that survived every historical 30 year retirement he tested, adjusting the amount for inflation each year, and the Trinity study later reframed the same idea as success rates across portfolios. Spending divided by 4% is the same as spending times 25, which is where the shortcut comes from. Newer work disagrees with itself in an instructive way: Morningstar's December 2025 base case for new retirees is 3.9%, Bengen's own 2025 update puts the figure at 4.7%, and plans that let spending flex down in bad markets can support more than either. So treat 4% as a starting frame, not a promise. One more caution that matters for us specifically: the rule is built on US market history, and historically it has worked poorly in many other countries. If you expect to retire in the UK, Canada, Ghana or Nigeria, your number deserves a more cautious rate and local thinking, not a borrowed American one.

Your first step: take last year's rough total spending, or your monthly essentials times 12 as a starting estimate, and multiply by 25. Write the result down somewhere private. It will be wrong in detail. It is still the first honest draft of your target, and every plan in the sections below hangs off it.

You are watching people announce offers while you are still applying, and every money conversation can feel like it is meant for someone whose life has already started. It is not. This framework is arguably most useful right now, before the money arrives, because the first paycheck has a way of disappearing into a lifestyle before you ever decide what you wanted it to do. You do not need income to set the goal, learn the accounts in your country, or decide your order of operations. You need income to fund it, and that part will come.

Two honest cautions while you wait. If you are on a student visa, your options for side income are limited by your status, and no hustle is worth risking that. And please do not invest borrowed money or money you need for applications, rent and food while you search. Cash and calm are worth more than any return right now.

Your first step: write the plan now, while it is still theoretical. Your goal number, your country's first account (Section 5), and what percentage of your first paycheck goes where. When the offer lands, you will be executing a decision you already made, not improvising one at your most emotional moment.

Four things that are not the same thing

A lot of cross-border mistakes, and I include some of mine, come from treating these four as one decision. They are separate:

Citizenship

Your passport. It affects some tax duties, for example US citizens are taxed on worldwide income wherever they live, and it affects which accounts you may open.

Tax residence

Where the tax authority treats you as resident, usually based on days present and ties. This decides where you report income and gains, and it can differ from your citizenship.

Investment jurisdiction

Where the account or asset legally sits. A Nigerian living in London can hold UK ISAs and Nigerian bonds at the same time. Each sits under different rules and different investor protection.

Goal currency

The currency you will actually spend the money in. School fees in dollars, a house in naira, retirement in pounds. Your returns only mean something measured in this currency. Section 6 shows why, with numbers.

02 · Budgeting and Tracking

A budget is a plan for payday, not a punishment

Let me show you the system I actually use, because it is the one thing on this site I did not just research, I live on it. It has four parts, and once it is running it takes me about 20 minutes a month. The month I stopped running it properly is the month my spending quietly drifted, and I did not notice until I closed the books and saw it in the numbers.

Pay yourself first

On payday, before anything else, money moves to savings and investing automatically. Whatever is left is what I live on. I learned this the obvious way. If you save what remains at the end of the month, the honest answer is usually nothing, because spending expands to fill whatever is sitting in the account.

Give every unit of money a job

My plan is zero based, which sounds stricter than it is. It just means income minus planned outgoings equals zero on paper before the month starts. Every amount has an assignment: bills, spending, sinking funds, savings, investing, and a small flex buffer for the things I know I will forget. Zero based does not mean spend everything. It means nothing is unplanned, including the saving.

Sinking funds, for the costs that were never surprises

Car insurance. Annual subscriptions. Christmas. Travel. Repairs. None of these are emergencies, they are just irregular, and they only feel like emergencies when we pretend they will not happen. Divide the yearly cost by the months until it is due and save that monthly. When the bill lands, the money is already sitting there and the month feels normal. This one habit removed most of the drama from my budget.

The monthly close, and the flag

Once a month I compare what actually happened to the plan. The totals matter less than you would think. The most useful part is flagging the charges that break the pattern: the subscription that quietly renewed at a higher price, the fee you were never told about, the category that jumped for no clear reason. Catching two or three of those a year pays for the whole habit. If you take one practice from this entire site, take that one.

If this is youYou just started full time work and the first real paychecks are landing

First, genuinely, congratulations. I remember the feeling. A salary hitting your account on a schedule is a different life from student money and holiday jobs, and it comes with a quiet trap. In the first three months, almost everyone I know upgraded everything at once. Nicer apartment, nicer everything, and by month four the bigger paycheck felt exactly as tight as the small one used to. Nothing was wrong with them. The increases just never got assigned, so they dissolved.

You do not have to live like a student forever, that is not the point and it is not sustainable either. The point is sequence. Let the first two or three paychecks land in a plan you wrote while you were still earning less. Fund the starter emergency fund, set the payday transfer, take the full employer match. Then upgrade your life deliberately, one thing at a time, with the upgraded form written into the plan. You will enjoy those upgrades more when they are chosen, not drifted into.

Your first step: before your next payday, copy the Budget Plan example below and fill it in with your real numbers, and set one automatic transfer for the day after payday. Even a small one. The habit is the asset. The amount can grow with you.

I keep one ledger, not five apps. Every transaction gets a date, a short description, a category from a fixed list I do not keep reinventing, and whether money came in or went out. Car costs all live in one place so I can see what the car truly costs me per month, because that number is always bigger than the fuel and payment suggest. At the end of the month I total each category, compare it to the plan, and write down anything that surprised me. Surprises are the data. A category that is over budget three months in a row is not a discipline problem, it is a plan that does not match my real life, so I change the plan honestly instead of feeling guilty monthly.

03 · Saving and Safety Net

The emergency fund, sized to your actual life

I want to say this plainly, because the standard advice can feel like it is mocking you. "Just save six months of expenses" is easy to write and hard to hear when you are starting from zero, supporting family, or still looking for work. So let us size it to your actual life instead. Essential spending means rent, food, transport, minimum payments and your phone, not your full lifestyle. And the right target depends on how fragile your income is, not on a number from the internet.

Your situationSensible targetWhy
Student with family support1 month, growing to 2Your risks are small and specific: a broken laptop, a flight home. Start small and real.
New grad, stable job, no dependants3 monthsOne income, but low fixed costs and you can cut spending fast if you lose the job.

Where cash lives

Emergency money has one job: be there, in full, on a bad day. That means an insured deposit account or an equivalent government backed savings product, easy to access, and separate from your everyday spending account so you do not drift into it on a good day. It does not mean investments, crypto, or money lent to a friend's business, however promising. A boring account is the entire point.

  • US: FDIC insured savings accounts, including high-yield savings accounts. Check coverage at fdic.gov.
  • UK: FSCS protected instant access savings or Cash ISAs. See fscs.org.uk.
  • Nigeria: NDIC insured bank deposits, or treasury bills for money you will not need immediately. See ndic.gov.ng and the country playbook below.
  • Canada: CDIC insured savings accounts. See cdic.ca.
  • Ireland, Ghana, Sweden: Check the deposit guarantee scheme linked in your country playbook before choosing where to park cash.

Rates on these accounts change constantly, so I am not listing them here, they would be stale before you read them. Compare current rates from the regulator or a reputable comparison source, and treat the interest as a small bonus. The point of this fund is stability. Return is someone else's job, later in the order.

04 · Investing Foundations

First, separate the wrapper from the asset

Almost every confused conversation I have had about investing, including confused conversations I started, traces back to one mix-up: a tax wrapper is not an asset. A 401(k), an ISA, a TFSA and an ISK are containers. They decide how tax is charged. What you put inside the container, a fund, a bond, cash, decides your return and your risk. A good container holding cash for 30 years still loses to inflation. A great fund in the wrong container can leak tax you never needed to pay. You need both decisions right, and they are two decisions.

The two US retirement accounts, and how the tax actually works

If you are taxed in the US, this is the pair of decisions most of your long term money will pass through, so it is worth understanding properly rather than copying what a coworker does. A 401(k) and a Roth IRA can hold the exact same funds. What differs is when the tax is paid, who controls the account, and how the money comes out. The limits below are the 2026 limits, from the IRS.

Pay tax now or pay tax later is the whole game. If your tax rate in retirement will be higher than it is today, paying now, the Roth route, usually wins. If today's rate is the higher one, for example in a peak earning year, deferring tax with the traditional route usually wins. Early in a career, when your salary and tax rate are often at their lowest point of your working life, that logic is why many people our age lean Roth first. It is a judgement about two tax rates, not a loyalty to an account.

Go deeperThe same $10,000, both routes, side by side

Take $10,000 of pre tax pay and a tax rate of 22% that stays the same in both directions, growing at 7% a year for 30 years. Growth turns $10,000 into about $76,100.

Traditional route: the full $10,000 goes in untaxed and grows to about $76,100. You withdraw it and pay 22% tax on the way out, leaving about $59,400.

Roth route: you pay 22% tax first, so $7,800 goes in. It grows by the same multiple to about $59,400, and it comes out tax free.

Same answer, and that is the honest insight most explainers skip. When the tax rate is identical, the order does not matter. The routes only separate when the rates differ. Roth wins if your retirement rate is higher than 22%. Traditional wins if it is lower. Anyone who tells you one is simply better, without asking about your tax rates now and later, is skipping the actual decision.

Where the match fits: before either comparison matters. Contribute enough to the 401(k) to capture the full employer match first, because the match is an instant return of roughly 50 to 100% on that slice of your pay, and no tax calculation beats an instant doubling. Employer money also does not count against your own $24,500 employee limit for 2026. It counts toward a separate, much larger overall plan ceiling. A common order people use after that is: full match, then the Roth IRA up to its limit for the flexibility and the tax free growth, then back to the 401(k) toward its much larger limit. Your order may differ, especially in a high tax year, but "match first" survives almost every form of the plan.

Your first step: find two numbers this week. Your current marginal tax rate, and the percentage you contribute to your 401(k) against the percentage your employer matches up to. If the second pair does not line up, fix that before you open anything new.

Go deeperThe backdoor Roth IRA, named and explained carefully

Once your income passes the phase out range, you cannot contribute to a Roth IRA directly. For 2026 that range is $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers, and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly. IRS, 2026 limits Higher earners, including people in our wider circle in software and other well paid roles who already max out a 401(k), use a two step route that has become known as the backdoor Roth IRA. There is no account with that name. It is two ordinary steps, done in the right order:

  1. Clear the trap first. If you hold pre tax money in any traditional, SEP, SIMPLE or rollover IRA, consider rolling it into a current employer's 401(k) that accepts roll ins, so your traditional IRA balance is zero on December 31 of the year you convert. That December 31 balance, not the balance on the day you convert, is the one the tax calculation uses. Your 401(k), your Roth accounts and your spouse's IRAs do not count in your calculation.
  2. Contribute, and do not deduct it. Put money into a traditional IRA as a nondeductible contribution, up to the $7,500 combined IRA limit for 2026. You need earned income at least equal to the contribution. There is no income limit on this step, or on converting.
  3. Convert, then invest. Move the balance into a Roth IRA. Any small earnings that appeared between contributing and converting are taxable. Then actually invest the money inside the Roth. Leaving it sitting in cash in the new account is a common and silent mistake.
  4. File Form 8606. This form is the official record of your already taxed basis, the proof that this money was taxed once and should not be taxed again on conversion. Skip it or file it for the wrong year and you invite confusion, notices, and the risk of paying tax twice on the same dollars.

The pro-rata rule, with the numbers that make it bite. The tax on a conversion is not calculated on just the money you moved. All your traditional IRAs are treated as one pool, and the tax free share of any conversion is your basis divided by the whole pool. Worked example: you hold $92,500 of pre tax rollover IRA money and add a $7,500 nondeductible contribution. The pool is $100,000 and only 7.5% of it is basis. Convert $7,500 and only 7.5% of it, $562.50, is tax free. The other $6,937.50 is taxable income, and the rest of your basis stays trapped inside the IRA for future years. Run the same move with a zero pre tax balance instead and the full $7,500 converts tax free, apart from any small earnings. Same route, opposite outcome, decided entirely by step one.

The mega backdoor Roth is a different animal. It does not live in an IRA at all. It lives inside an employer 401(k). You max your normal employee contributions, then add extra after tax, non Roth, employee contributions, then move that money to Roth, either by an in plan conversion or by an in service withdrawal into a Roth IRA. It only exists if your plan allows both of those features, voluntary after tax contributions and a way to convert or withdraw them while you still work there. Most plans do not offer both, so this is a question for your plan documents, not a right you can claim. For 2026, the overall ceiling on everything going into a plan like this, your contributions plus your employer's, is $72,000. So the theoretical mega backdoor room is $72,000 minus your $24,500, minus whatever your employer puts in: $37,500 of room if your employer adds $10,000, less if they add more.

This is general education about how the mechanism works, not a recommendation to use it. Conversions cannot be undone. If your income puts you near this territory, the year you first do it is a good year to pay for an hour of proper tax advice.

Before you buy anything, answer these

One of you asked me the best form of this question: when I buy a stock, why am I buying it, and how do I know it is the right decision? This checklist is my honest answer. If you cannot answer these, you do not have a decision yet, you have a feeling. Feelings are allowed. They are just not a strategy.

  • Why am I buying this, in one sentence I could say out loud?
  • What has to happen for this to make money, and what has to happen for it to lose?
  • When will I need this money? Anything needed within about 3 years does not belong in the stock market.
  • What are the total costs: fees, spreads, FX conversion, tax?
  • Who holds my asset, and are they regulated? Can I verify that myself?
  • If this fell 30% and stayed there for two years, would I still hold it? If not, it is too big.

A few of you asked for reliable medium to long term options beyond ETFs, and how to actually make those calls. Fair question, so let me be straight about the trade. Individual stocks, bonds you pick yourself, property, a business: these can all work, and some of us will do well with them. What they ask of you is work. Research before, attention during, and the honesty to admit when the original reason you bought is gone. An index fund asks almost nothing of you after payday, which is exactly why it is the default I suggest while life is busy. It is not that the other routes are wrong. Fees feel small. They are not.

On a pot growing for 40 years, the difference between paying 0.2% a year in fees and paying 1.2% a year is roughly a quarter to a third of your final pot, given away in slices too small to notice each year. You cannot control markets. Nobody can, whatever their confidence on a podcast. Fees are one of the few things you fully control, so control them. Prefer low-cost diversified funds, and ask for the total annual cost of anything you are sold, in writing, before you sign.

Bonds and cash, properly: the part of the plan that lets you sleep

Stocks get the attention, but bonds and cash are what most goals under five years should actually be made of, and they deserve more than a passing mention. A bond is a loan you make, to a government or a company. You are paid interest, and you are promised your money back at a set date. The risks are that the borrower fails, or that you need to sell early at whatever price the market offers that day.

Go deeperTreasury bills, bond ladders, and why big funds buy African bonds

Treasury bills are the shortest form: lending to a government for weeks or months, bought at a small discount and repaid in full at the end. They are the usual home for money with a near date, like a deposit or school fees due next year. A bond ladder is simply owning bonds that mature in different years, one after another, so part of your money comes back each year to spend or reinvest, instead of everything riding on a single date and a single interest rate.

Nigeria, in both currencies. FGN bonds and treasury bills pay naira interest, and the dated example in the Nigeria playbook shows the shape of recent offers: the November 2026 FGN Savings Bond paid 13.25% for 2 years and 14.25% for 3 years. That offer has closed and new ones replace it monthly, so treat those figures as a dated example of the level, not a standing rate. Eurobonds are Nigerian bonds priced in dollars instead. They remove the naira exchange rate risk from the return, and in exchange they usually pay less than the naira equivalent and move in price with global markets. Dollar funds offered by licensed Nigerian managers sit in a similar place: dollar exposure, professional management, and fees and platform risk to check before you commit.

Why do large global funds buy African government bonds at all? Yield. When a US or European government bond pays a low single digit rate and a Nigerian or Ghanaian bond pays well into double digits in local currency, that gap is tempting to a fund hunting return. But the gap is not free money appearing from nowhere. It is the market pricing in inflation, currency falls and political risk. Sometimes the buyer still comes out ahead. Sometimes the currency fall eats the entire extra yield and more, which is exactly the calculation in Section 6. When you see a high rate quoted, always ask: in which currency, against what inflation, and what has to go right for me to keep it.

Cash has a job description too. A high-yield savings account might pay a few percent in a good year, while inflation runs at about 3%. At best, savings roughly keep pace. They do not build wealth, and they were never meant to. Their job is certainty for near money. For a goal two or three years away, a certificate of deposit, where you lock cash for a fixed term at a fixed rate, can pay a little more than easy access savings in exchange for that lock. Match the term to the goal date, and never lock away your emergency fund.

Your first step: list your goals with dates under five years. For each one, ask whether it is currently sitting in something that can fall 20% in a bad year. If it is, that goal is a candidate for bills, bonds or a CD instead, and Section 3 is where to rehouse it.

Beyond index funds: the tools, and the honest risks

Some of you want to go further than funds, and I would rather explain the tools plainly than leave you to learn them from a feed. None of what follows is a recommendation. It is a map of what these things are, so that nothing in a trading app is a mystery button to you.

Go deeperOrder types, and realised versus unrealised loss

When you buy or sell on a platform, the instruction you give is called an order, and the type matters more than most app screens suggest. A market order says do it now, at the best available price. It almost always fills, and it guarantees nothing about the price. A limit order says do it only at my price or better. It guarantees the price, and it may never fill at all if the price never reaches yours. Definitions follow FINRA and Investor.gov order type guidance

A stop order, often labelled a stop loss, sits dormant until the price trades at or through your stop level, and then it becomes a market order. Read that twice, because it is the part people miss: a stop does not guarantee its price, it guarantees a sale at whatever price comes next. Worked example: you own a stock at $50 and set a stop at $45. Bad earnings land after hours and the stock opens at $38. Your stop triggers and sells as a market order near $38, not $45. A stop limit order fixes the price problem and creates the opposite one: at the stop it becomes a limit order at your limit price, so in that same gap it may never fill, and you still own the falling stock. A trailing stop follows the highest price reached, by a fixed amount or percentage, rising as the price rises and never falling back, then triggers in exactly the same way. For a long term holder, treat stops as trading tools rather than insurance: ordinary wobbles can trigger them and lock in a loss, gaps defeat the price you had in mind, and the sale itself can create tax. Knowing what they do is the protection. Using them is optional.

Realised and unrealised loss is the concept underneath a lot of panic. If something you own falls in price and you hold it, the loss is unrealised, a number on a screen. It becomes realised only when you sell. That is genuinely useful to understand, because it means a fall is not yet a fact about your money, and long term investors often ride out falls for years. It cuts the other way too, and this part matters just as much: some things never come back. A single company can fail completely, and "it is only a loss when I sell" has kept people holding worthless positions all the way to zero. The idea protects you from panic selling a diversified fund. It does not rescue a bad pick.

Go deeperOptions and short selling, explained plainly, with the risks in front

An option is a contract, and one contract normally controls 100 shares. That is why the prices look deceptively small: premiums are quoted per share. Investor.gov's own example makes it concrete: a premium of $2.20 means $220 per contract, paid up front and never refunded. A call option is the right, not the obligation, to buy at a set price, the strike, before a set date. A put option is the same right, to sell. The buyer pays the premium. The seller, called the writer, receives it and takes on the obligation if the buyer exercises. Part of every premium is time value, and time value melts to zero at expiry, so you can be right about the direction and still lose, because the move came too late or too small. Mechanics follow Investor.gov, An Introduction to Options

What can be lost, stated exactly, because this is where people get hurt. Buy a call or a put and the most you can lose is the premium, 100% of it, and that outcome is common rather than freakish: every contract that expires out of the money does exactly that. Sell a covered call and your upside is capped at the strike while you still carry the stock's fall. Sell a cash secured put and you can lose close to the full strike times 100 shares if the stock collapses. Sell a naked call, a call on shares you do not own, and the loss is unlimited, because there is no highest price a share can reach. Options can be used carefully, to insure a position with a bought put for example, but in practice most beginners use them as leveraged bets with an expiry date attached, which is why accounts get emptied by them so reliably.

Short selling means borrowing shares, selling them, and hoping to buy them back cheaper later to return them. If the price rises instead, your loss has no ceiling, for the same reason: a stock can keep rising indefinitely, and the SEC says so in almost those words. Add the carrying costs people forget: borrow fees, any dividends you owe the lender while you are short, the lender's right to recall the shares and force you to buy back at the worst moment, and margin calls that can arrive on short notice and force the issue. A crowded short can also squeeze violently upward as every short buyer scrambles for the same shares. I am explaining these because you will encounter people using them, sometimes loudly and profitably for a season. My honest position: unless this is close to your actual profession, treat both as spectator knowledge. Knowing what they are protects you. Using them is a job with real tuition fees, paid in your own money.

Your first step: if you already use any of these tools, write down, in one sentence each, what has to happen for the position to make money and the most it can lose. If the second half of that sentence surprises you, close the position down to a size that would not.

Go deeperFinancial advisors: what they are, and how to use one well

An advisor is a professional who reviews your whole position, taxes, accounts, insurance, goals, and helps you build and maintain a plan. Two things people often do not realise. First, the dedicated, named advisor at a big bank is a balance tier, not a starting point. The published thresholds, checked November 2026: Chase Private Client starts at $150,000 in linked balances, Citigold at $200,000, and Wells Fargo Premier at $250,000. Tiers like these buy you a named banker, fee waivers and access to an advisor. They are not the same thing as paying for fiduciary advice, and most of us are below all of them for now, which is fine: a low cost diversified portfolio, plus a one off paid consultation before a big move, covers most early career needs. Second, you do not need a fortune to ask questions. Hourly and flat fee planners exist precisely for people our age.

The standard they work to matters more than their title. A registered investment adviser owes you a fiduciary duty: loyalty and care, across the whole relationship, with conflicts eliminated or fully disclosed. A broker dealer works under the SEC's Regulation Best Interest, in force since June 2020: recommendations must be in your best interest at the moment they are made, without the firm's interest put ahead of yours. That is stronger than the old suitability rule you may still see quoted, which only required a recommendation to be suitable, but it is not an ongoing fiduciary duty to monitor your account. The same person can wear both hats in different accounts, which is why the short relationship summary every firm must hand you, Form CRS, is worth actually reading. You can verify any adviser free on the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site and any broker on FINRA BrokerCheck.

What it typically costs. A human advisor charging on assets commonly takes about 1% a year, with published ranges roughly 0.25% to 1.5% depending on balance and service. On $500,000 growing at 7% for 25 years, a 1% fee turns roughly $2.7 million into roughly $2.1 million. The advice may well be worth that to the right person, but go in knowing the price in money, not just in percent. Fee only alternatives: hourly advice commonly runs about $200 to $400 an hour, and one off plans a few thousand. Robo advisors charge about 0.25% a year at the best known names, with Vanguard's digital advice around 0.15% net, fund fees extra in every case. And learn one distinction that protects you: fee only means fees from you alone. Fee based can mean fees from you plus commissions from products.

Five questions to ask any advisor before you hand over anything:

  1. Are you a fiduciary to me, at all times, and will you put that in writing? And in which capacity, advisory or brokerage, for this account?
  2. How are you paid, in total, on my money: asset fees, commissions, product incentives, all of it, expressed as a yearly cost on my actual balance?
  3. What are your qualifications and disciplinary history? Then verify the answer yourself rather than taking it on trust.
  4. What will you actually do for me, how often will we meet, and what will you not do?
  5. What would you put me in, and why is it better than a low cost diversified index portfolio after your fee? A good advisor can answer this specifically, including, when it is true, "you do not need me yet".

A good advisor answers all five calmly and in writing. Vagueness on the second one is your answer.

Go deeperPooling money, agriculture, and building your own thing

There is a route into investing none of us can do alone at 23, and it is worth naming honestly. A house deposit, a duplex to house hack together, land, a small business: these have minimum sizes that shut out a single early salary but open up to four or five people pooling deliberately. Investment clubs and informal pooling groups exist for exactly this, and the mathematics is real. A larger sum, deployed well, buys assets none of the members could reach separately, and shared research is usually better research.

The risks are just as real, so hear them in the same breath. Money pooled with friends is friendship put under load. People's circumstances change at different speeds, someone will need out early, and a disagreement about an asset you cannot quickly sell can cost the relationship, not just the return. If a group of you ever does this, do the unglamorous parts first and in writing: who decides, how someone exits and at what valuation, what happens if someone stops contributing, and where the records live. Get that written while everyone still likes each other. This is education about a route some people take, not a recommendation to take it, and it is nobody's business on this site to recruit you into any particular group or project, mine included.

The formal versions, so you recognise them when you meet them. A US investment club is usually a general partnership, sometimes formed so informally that the members do not realise that is what they are. It needs an Employer Identification Number from the IRS, a written partnership agreement covering contributions, voting, joining, leaving and valuation, and it files Form 1065 each year as an information return. The partnership itself pays no federal income tax. Instead every member receives a Schedule K-1 and reports their share of dividends, interest and gains on their own tax return. Contributions are usually tracked in units, exactly like a mutual fund: the club's total value divided by units outstanding gives a unit price, a new contribution buys units at that price, and a departing member is paid their units times the current value. That unit system is what keeps members who joined at different times treated fairly. One more line worth knowing: a club where everyone actively takes part in decisions is treated differently from a passive pool run by one person for others. The moment members are passive and relying on one person's efforts, securities law starts paying attention.

Buying property together through an LLC sounds tidier than it is. The LLC itself solves almost nothing. The operating agreement is the real product, and it has to answer, in writing: who puts in what and owns what share, who guarantees the mortgage, because lenders to a new LLC usually demand personal guarantees, which means members can be exposed together despite the words "limited liability"; what happens when someone stops paying and a capital call lands; who manages tenants and at what spending limit; and, the section everyone skips, the exit. Buy sell terms, a right of first refusal, how the property gets valued, what happens on death, divorce, bankruptcy or simple loss of interest, and how a deadlock gets broken. Without those clauses, a minority share in a friend owned LLC holding one house is close to unsellable. There is no market for it, and you usually cannot force a sale of the house. Multi member LLCs also default to partnership tax treatment, which means another Form 1065, more K-1s, and the possibility of being taxed on income the LLC kept in the bank rather than paid out to you.

Real estate syndications are the grown up, stranger version of the same idea: a sponsor finds and runs a deal, passive investors supply most of the money. Most run under the SEC's Regulation D. Rule 506(b) allows no public advertising and a limited number of non accredited investors who must be sophisticated. Rule 506(c) allows advertising but only verified accredited investors may invest. Accredited currently means income over $200,000, or $300,000 jointly with a spouse, in each of the last two years with a reasonable expectation of the same this year, or net worth over $1,000,000 excluding your main home, or certain professional licences. Filing a Form D is not SEC approval of anything. Typical economics, so you can read an offering document without flinching: an acquisition fee of about 1 to 3% of the price, an asset management fee of about 1 to 2% a year, and a promote, the sponsor taking roughly 20 to 30% of profits above a preferred return, often 7 to 8%. Your money is typically locked up for 3 to 10 years, distributions can pause, extensions are the sponsor's call, and borrowing against the property magnifies losses as well as gains. And one honest gap: there is no reliable, comprehensive dataset of how syndications perform or fail overall, so anyone quoting you a precise success or loss rate for the whole category is marketing, not informing.

A friend's startup, or a small farm, against an index fund. Keep the base rates in front of you. US Bureau of Labor Statistics survival data for new businesses generally: roughly 80% survive the first year, about 50% are still going at year 5, and about 35% at year 10. You may have heard that 90% of startups fail. That figure has no traceable primary source, so do not use it, in either direction. Surviving is also not the same as investors making money: a business can live for years and return nothing to the people who funded it. A private venture gives you no market price, no daily ability to sell, and paperwork that decides everything, and friends and family rounds often lack the protections professionals negotiate for themselves. Compare that honestly with a diversified index fund: priced every day, sellable every day, and its worst historical stretches are knowable before you start.

Agriculture comes up in our circle often, and not by accident. Many of us have family roots in it and can see the gap between how food is produced back home and how it could be. As an investment it behaves like what it is, a real operating business with seasons: land, inputs, labour, weather, pests, commodity prices and buyers all have to line up, and returns arrive on farming time, not app time. It rewards people who are close to the operation or partnered with someone who is, and it punishes distant, hands off money more often than the pitch decks admit. One red flag deserves its own sentence: farming income is variable by nature, so a scheme promising a guaranteed high fixed monthly return from agriculture is describing something farming cannot honestly promise. Check the operator's registration with the relevant regulator before any money moves. If you do back a friend's venture or a farm, size it as money you can lose entirely without losing the friendship or the decade, demand written terms and a reporting rhythm, and keep the boring base in Section 1 running underneath it.

And then there is building your own venture. Starting something, a business, a product, a practice, is the highest risk route on this entire page and also the one with no ceiling. It can return nothing for years and then change your life in a way no fund will. Both things are true at once. The slow, disciplined path in the sections above is not the opposite of that bet. For most builders, it is the floor underneath it: the match still captured, the emergency fund intact, a small boring investment still running, so that if the venture fails you have lost a chapter and not the decade. Section 8 takes up how to structure that bet on yourself.

Your first step: if pooling or a venture is already on your mind, write down which of the two you mean, the amount, and the worst realistic outcome if it returns nothing. A dream that survives being written down that plainly is worth a longer conversation.

06 · Diaspora and FX

A return is only real in the currency you will spend it in

This section exists because of us specifically. Several of you asked about dollar investing from Nigeria, and about money back home generally, and I have had to learn this one properly because my own goals sit in more than one currency. Three different numbers all get called "return", and mixing them up is expensive:

  • Nominal return: the percentage the investment itself reports, in its own currency.
  • Real return: nominal return minus inflation where the asset sits. Earning 14% where inflation is 20% means going backwards, whatever the statement says.
  • Return in your goal currency: what is left after converting back into the currency your goal is priced in. For those of us investing across borders, this is the only number that decides whether the goal got closer.

Worked example, run the numbers yourself

You invest back home and it grows +20% in local currency over a year. In the same year, that currency falls 25% against your goal currency, say the dollar.

Your money in dollar terms: 1.20 × 0.75 = 0.90. That is about -10%, before fees, transfer costs and tax. The local statement says you won. Your goal says you lost. Both are true, and only one of them pays for the goal. Try your own numbers in the comparator right below this card.

Your return in goal currency terms: about -10%

None of this means never invest back home. I want to be careful here, because for many of us home is not an abstraction, it is family, land, obligations and a future we may genuinely return to. It means price the currency risk in, compare honestly against investing where you live, and match the currency to the goal. Money for a goal in Nigeria belongs largely in naira assets. Money for a goal in dollars should not quietly depend on the naira holding its value.

If this is youYou are in Nigeria, or earning in naira, and you want dollar exposure

Dollar assets carry their own risks, US market falls included, platforms that let you buy them charge spreads and fees that are easy to miss, and a dollar return can still disappoint once you convert back at a worse rate than you planned, or once tax in either country takes its share.

FGN bonds and treasury bills exist, they pay real rates, and if your future spending is in naira they match your goal currency, which this whole section argues is the main thing. The question is never naira or dollars as an identity. It is which goal, priced in which currency, by when.

07 · Real Estate

Renting is not throwing money away. Buying is not automatically smart.

I know the pressure on this one. A house feels like the certificate that says you have arrived, and for those of us with family watching from home, it carries even more weight than the money. So let us take the emotion seriously and still do the math honestly. Rent buys you flexibility at exactly the age when your career may move you between cities and countries. Buying tends to make sense when you expect to stay put for roughly five years or more, your job is stable, and the all-in monthly cost is genuinely comparable to renting somewhere you would actually live. Not the mortgage payment alone. All in.

The costs buyers forget

  • Property taxes and, where it applies, HOA or service charges, every year, rising over time.
  • Insurance, and maintenance. A working rule is to budget around 1% of the property value per year for upkeep.
  • Closing costs when you buy and agent and legal costs when you sell. Moving house is expensive at both ends, which is why the five year question matters.
  • The deposit years: money locked in a deposit is money not invested. That opportunity cost is real, even if it never appears on a statement.

House hacking, with eyes open

House hacking means buying a place, living in part of it, and renting the rest, in a duplex or by renting spare rooms, so tenants cover much of the mortgage. One of you is seriously looking at a first house in the US, and this is the form of buying I find most interesting at our age, because done well it can cut your housing cost dramatically. The cautions are equally real, and I would be doing you a disservice to skip them:

  • Owner-occupancy rules are real. Low deposit mortgages for house hackers are priced for people who genuinely live in the property, and the rules say so. With a US FHA loan you must move in within 60 days of signing and intend to keep living there for at least a year. Buying with the quiet intention of renting the whole place from day one is occupancy fraud, not a loophole. After the year, moving out and renting your old unit is a different and generally permitted story.
  • Insurance and permissions follow the tenants. Tell your insurer the rooms or units are rented. A standard homeowner policy may exclude tenant caused loss or liability, and lenders and insurers commonly expect a landlord endorsement or a dwelling policy once rent is coming in. Short term rental is frequently excluded outright and can clash with loan terms, HOA rules and local law at the same time. Local landlord licensing and tenancy law apply to house hackers too.
  • Vacancy is real. Tenants leave. Budget for empty months instead of assuming full rent forever.
  • Repairs are real, and they are yours. A boiler does not care that the rent was covering the mortgage.
  • You become a landlord, with legal duties to your tenants that vary by city and country. Learn them before, not after.
If this is youYou are torn between paying off student loans and saving for a house

This exact question came in from our group, so let me work it the way I would work it with you on a call. It feels like a fork in the road, but it is usually a sequence. First, the match and the starter emergency fund come before both, because a house deposit with no cushion is how people end up house rich and one repair away from a credit card. Second, look at your loan's actual interest rate and what kind of loan it is. In the US, federal loans carry protections private ones do not. In the UK, student loans are repaid through the tax system as a percentage of income above a threshold, which makes them behave more like a graduate tax than a normal debt, and rushing to overpay them is often the wrong move. Third, compare honestly: paying a loan at, say, 7% is a guaranteed 7% return. A house deposit is not competing with investing in general, it is competing with that guaranteed number and with your timeline for actually buying.

There is also a human answer inside the math. If the debt sits on your mind every single day, clearing it faster has a value no spreadsheet will show you, and I will not pretend otherwise. Just make it a chosen trade, with the numbers in front of you, not a guilt payment.

Go deeperHouse hacking, with the actual numbers on the table

How people actually buy these with small deposits, US rules. An FHA loan allows 3.5% down on a 1 to 4 unit property with a credit score of 580 or above, with mortgage insurance premiums on top, and pure investment properties are not eligible. For 3 and 4 unit FHA purchases there is an extra gate, a self sufficiency test: 75% of the total market rent across all units, including yours, has to cover the full monthly payment, principal, interest, tax, insurance and mortgage insurance. Expect to hold reserves as well, commonly around three months of payments on multi unit purchases. The conventional route changed recently in buyers' favour: since November 2023, Fannie Mae allows 5% down on owner occupied 2 to 4 unit homes within standard loan limits, where 15 to 25% used to be required, with private mortgage insurance above 80% borrowed. Loan limits change yearly and vary by county, so check the current limit for the specific property rather than trusting any national figure, mine included.

Your first step: if you are house hunting now, take one real listing and write its full monthly cost, mortgage, tax, insurance and a maintenance allowance, next to the rent the rooms would realistically bring in your city, not the optimistic figure. That single honest page settles most of the debate.

Go deeperBuilding and land in Nigeria, with ranges and the caveats that matter

Many of us will be asked about this by family if we have not been already, so here are the shapes of numbers that come up, stated as illustrations of the scale involved, not promises. Building a three storey house in a developing area can run to roughly 150 million naira in build costs, and finished houses of that kind in the right area have sold in the region of 300 million naira. Land bought well, say around 20 million naira, in an area that develops over two to three years, has in cases been sold on for around 50 million naira. I am showing you those figures so the conversations at home stop being abstract.

Now the caveats, and they are not fine print, they are most of the investment. Start with what a C of O actually is. Under the Land Use Act 1978, land in each state is held by the Governor in trust, and a Certificate of Occupancy is evidence of a right of occupancy granted by the Governor, in practice a long leasehold, commonly described as 99 years, not freehold ownership. In the FCT the same role sits with the FCT Minister. And a genuine C of O does not transfer itself: section 22 of the Act makes it unlawful to assign, mortgage or sublease a statutory right of occupancy without the Governor's Consent first obtained. Every resale needs fresh consent, or the transfer is defective however much was paid. Verification is a sequence, in this order, before you pay anything beyond a refundable deposit held by your own lawyer:

  1. Instruct your own lawyer. Independent of the seller, the developer, the agent, and ideally of the family member helping you. Photocopies supplied by a seller prove nothing. Convincing fake C of Os, deeds, consents and survey plans are a documented staple of land fraud.
  2. Search the registry yourself, through your lawyer. In Lagos that is the Lands Bureau and Land Registry at Alausa, Ikeja. In Abuja it is AGIS and the FCTA Department of Land Administration. Confirm the C of O exists, the registered holder matches the seller, the plot description matches, and there are no mortgages, caveats, court orders or government notices against it. Get a certified true copy of the title from the registry, not from the seller.
  3. Chart the survey. Take the survey plan to the Office of the Surveyor General and have it charted. This confirms the plot is where the documents say it is, and, critically, whether it is free from government acquisition. Land under acquisition can be reclaimed regardless of what you paid. If the title rests on an excision or a Gazette, confirm this specific plot falls inside the excised area.
  4. Check the whole chain of title. Every earlier transfer needed its own Governor's Consent. A break in that chain, a C of O still in a deceased person's name with no probate behind it, or family land sold by one branch of the family without the others, are all live defects that surface after your money is gone.
  5. Do the physical and community checks. Visit, or send someone independent and trusted, with a surveyor to confirm the beacons on the ground. Ask neighbours and the community head about disputes. This is where omonile trouble shows up: members of landowning families claiming the family never sold, or sold without proper consent, then demanding successive settlement payments and disrupting building work. Clean government paper alone does not settle a live customary dispute. In Lagos, also check the agent or developer against LASRERA, the state real estate regulator.
  6. Perfect your own purchase properly. Deed of Assignment, stamped, Governor's Consent obtained, then registered. Budget the perfection costs on top of the price, and get the current assessment for your specific transaction from the Lands Bureau rather than trusting any percentage quoted in a blog post or a sales pitch.

The scams to name plainly: the same plot sold twice, or five times. Government acquired land sold by someone with no right to sell it. Forged documents that survive a casual look. Agents and developers who are unregistered, or who pressure you to pay in full before search results come back. And the diaspora specific ones: paying through relatives with no receipts, and building with no staged payments tied to verified progress, no independent quantity surveyor, and no dated photo and video evidence, then discovering substituted materials or a different plot when you land. People: who is actually on the ground for you, who supervises the builder week by week, and what happens to your money if that person stops answering the phone? Distance: managing a build from another country is slow, stressful and expensive in attention, and that cost belongs in your calculation alongside the naira figures. None of this means do not do it. Families in our circle have built real wealth this way. It means the return lives or dies on verification and supervision, not on the spread between 150 and 300.

The UK version of house hacking, since one of you asked: the classic US move, a small multifamily building on a low deposit owner occupier loan, does not translate neatly. Ordinary UK residential mortgages restrict letting, and houses in multiple occupation carry their own licensing and mortgage products. The closest simple equivalent is a lodger in a home you live in, and the UK has a scheme built for exactly that. Under the Rent a Room Scheme, you can earn up to £7,500 a year tax free from letting a furnished room in your only or main home. The limit is £3,750 each if someone else in the home also receives letting income. The test runs on gross receipts, rent plus anything charged for meals, cleaning or bills. At or under the limit the relief is automatic. Above it, you file a tax return and choose the better of being taxed on receipts minus £7,500 or receipts minus your actual expenses. The scheme does not cover unfurnished rooms, a home converted into separate self contained flats, or a home you have moved out of. GOV.UK and HMRC Helpsheet HS223, checked November 2026

The legal shape matters as much as the tax. In England, a lodger who lives with you and shares your kitchen, bathroom or living room is usually an excluded occupier: a licence rather than a tenancy, ended with reasonable notice, usually one rent period, with no court order needed. Someone who shares no living space with you gets more protection: formal written notice, and a court order if they refuse to leave, and calling them a lodger on paper does not change which one they are. The living arrangement decides. Practical gates before you rely on any of this: most residential mortgage terms require you to tell your lender, and often get consent, before taking a lodger. A leasehold flat adds your lease and possibly the freeholder as a second gate, and many city first purchases are exactly that, so read the lease before exchange, not after. Tell your insurer too, since non disclosure can void cover, and expect a council tax single person discount to disappear once another adult lives there. The honest verdict: £7,500 a year is £625 a month, a serious offset against a mortgage in the early years when interest dominates the payment, and in expensive cities a spare room can rent for more than the tax free limit, with the excess taxable. Model it properly for a home you already want to buy. Do not buy a more expensive home in order to do it.

Your first step: for any land or build already being discussed with family, ask for the title documents and check whether a C of O exists and can be verified independently. That one request, made early and calmly, tells you most of what you need to know about the opportunity.

Renting well in the meantime

A common guardrail is to keep rent at or below about 30% of take-home pay, and to treat every amount below that as fuel for investing. The cheapest rent move is usually location and housemates, not negotiation: an extra 10 minutes of commute, or one more housemate for two years, can quietly be worth more than a pay rise. I say that as someone who knows rent is also about dignity and rest, not just optimisation. Spend what you need to live well. Just know the number you are choosing.

Do this todayWork out your rent as a percentage of take-home pay. If it is well above 30%, your next housing move matters more than any investment pick. If you are house hunting, price one property with every cost above included, then try the Rent or Buy tool in Section 11 before you fall in love with it.

08 · Earning More

Sometimes the fastest budget fix is a second income

Every budget has a floor. You can only cut so far before you are cutting things that make life worth living, and I am not interested in a plan that requires you to be miserable efficiently. Earning more has no floor. The side hustles that survive, in my observation, are usually built on things you already like and can already do: tutoring what you studied, a skill from your day job, fixing, coaching, writing or making content about something you genuinely enjoy. Liking it is not soft advice. The first months pay badly, sometimes embarrassingly, and interest is the only thing that carries most people through them.

Execution is cheaper than it has ever been

AI tools have cut the cost of the boring parts: drafting, design, bookkeeping, basic websites, customer replies. Work that needed a small team or an agency budget five years ago can now be tested by one person on weekends. That lowers the cost of finding out whether an idea works. It does not remove the need for a real customer with a real problem, so sell first and polish later. A paying stranger teaches you more in a week than a perfect plan teaches you in a year.

If this is youYou are studying in Europe and you need income alongside the degree

This one is for the person doing a master's in Ireland or elsewhere in Europe, watching savings drain while the degree is still a year from paying anything back. I see you, and the maths of that season is genuinely tight. Start with what your status allows: students on visas usually have a capped number of work hours during term, and those rules are not suggestions, so check your exact permission before you accept anything. Inside those hours, the highest value use of your time is usually work connected to your field, tutoring, research assistance, lab or teaching support, freelance work in the skill you are studying. It pays better than generic shifts and it compounds, because it is also experience on the CV when you graduate into the job market.

One more thing, said with love. In this season, protecting the degree is protecting the income. A side hustle that costs you the qualification it was meant to support is the most expensive money you will ever earn.

Your first step: check your permitted work hours today, in writing, from your university's international office or the official immigration site. Then list three field related services you could offer this month and message one potential customer before the week ends.

For the risk takers: bet on yourself, with a floor

Some of us will build businesses, and for them the highest return investment available is honestly themselves. That can be the right call, and I am not here to talk anyone out of it. I am here to say do it with structure:

  • Keep the employer match and a basic diversified investment running quietly in the background, even if the amount is small. If the bet fails, you have not lost the decade with it.
  • Decide the split in advance: for example, most of your investable money into the business for a defined 12 to 24 months, a minority into structured investing, reviewed on a date you set now, while you are calm.
  • Keep the emergency fund untouchable. Business risk and life risk should never share the same pot.
  • Track it like an investor would: money in, hours in, revenue out. If the numbers say stop, that is information, not failure. The people who scare me are not the ones whose first idea failed. They are the ones who never looked at the numbers.
Do this todayList three skills people already ask you for help with. People telling you what you are good at is free market research. Pick the one you enjoy most and work out the smallest paid form of it you could offer this month.

09 · Spending Well and Giving Well

Spend on purpose, including on time and on other people

This is the section most finance content skips, and it might be the one I care about most. A plan that only ever says "spend less" will eventually be abandoned, because we are people, not spreadsheets. So let us talk about spending well, and about giving, which for a lot of us is not optional decoration. It is family. It is faith. It is who we are trying to become.

What is your hour worth?

Work out a rough value for your time: your annual income divided by the hours you work, or simply what an extra hour of your skill earns in overtime or freelance work. Then use it as a lens, not a law. Paying for a cleaner, a shorter commute or a direct flight is not laziness if it buys back hours you will genuinely use for rest, family or higher value work, and if the base in Sections 1 to 4 is already funded. The traps are buying time and spending it on nothing, or buying it before you can afford it. In that order, those two traps catch almost everyone.

If this is youYou save a lot, and honestly, you are a bit afraid to spend on anything

One of you told me you over save, and I have thought about it a lot since, because I recognise it in myself too. You hit every target, the accounts are healthy, and still every purchase feels like a small failure, so the money grows and the life stays on pause. Here is what I have come to believe. Saving is a tool for buying a life, not a score to maximise. Money you never spend on time, health, people or joy is not being wise. It is being stored for a future you that you keep postponing meeting.

The question that helped me is the hour question above. Once you know roughly what your time is worth, some spending stops looking like waste and starts looking like trade. Paying someone to clean so your one free evening is actually free. Living ten minutes closer so you get an hour of your day back, every day. Flying direct to see family instead of losing a day to connections you will spend recovering from. Those are not leaks in the plan. For a person whose base is funded, they are the plan working.

Your first step: calculate your rough hourly number today, then look at one recurring drain on your time and ask what buying it back would actually cost per month. If your emergency fund and match are in place, give yourself a guilt free spending line in the budget, a named amount, and spend it on purpose for one month. Watch what happens to your relationship with the rest of the plan. It usually gets stronger, not weaker.

Giving, in a way that actually counts

Many of us support family back home, give through church or community, or want our giving to genuinely change something. Three principles have helped me hold this together without it quietly eating the rest of the plan:

  • Plan it like a budget line. A fixed monthly amount, decided in advance, protects both the giving and your own goals. Unplanned giving is where budgets quietly die, and then the giving becomes resentment, which helps nobody.
  • Effectiveness varies enormously. For charitable giving, well evaluated charities can do many times more good per unit of currency than average ones. Evaluators such as GiveWell publish their research openly if you want to compare before you give.
  • Consider a pledge. Some people commit a fixed percentage of income to effective giving, for life or for a set period. A small percentage started in your twenties is a habit, not a sacrifice, and habits are what we are building everywhere on this site.
  • Family support is real giving too. Be honest, with them and yourself, about what you can sustain. And where you can, give in ways that build independence, school fees, business capital, a skill, over open ended consumption support that has to be repeated forever and helps less each time.
If this is youYou want to give, and you want it to genuinely count

This question came from someone in our group who thinks about pledges and impact more seriously than most people think about their salary, so here is the honest account. Wanting your giving to count is not cold. It is the opposite. It means you believe the money is real help for real people, so it deserves the same care you would give an investment. The research on this is humbling: the difference between an average charity and a highly effective one is not a few percent, it can be many times over, for the same amount given. Same generosity, wildly different outcomes.

Hold two things at once, because both are true. Some of your giving will be relational, family, community, the people whose names you know, and it does not need an impact report to be right. And some of your giving can be deliberately aimed where evidence says a unit of currency does the most good in the world. Splitting those two pots consciously, and reviewing the split once a year, is a more honest system than drifting.

Your first step: set your giving as a named monthly line in your budget plan, at whatever amount is honest right now, and split it on paper into "people I know" and "most good I can find". Then spend twenty minutes on GiveWell's research before your next donation, just to see what the evidence based end of giving looks like.

The cheapest research is asking people like you

Here is a worked example of something this whole site is quietly built on. Big recurring bills are rarely fixed prices. They are starting prices, and the discount usually goes to whoever took an hour to compare and ask around. Three examples, from the kind of month anyone in our group might have.

  • Car insurance: young drivers get quoted painful numbers, and the spread between insurers for the same driver and the same car can be enormous. In one real case in our wider circle, asking peers of the same age what they were paying, then getting quotes from the insurers they named, took a renewal from over $3,000 a year to about $1,500 a year. Same driver, same cover, about $1,500 saved, for the price of a few calls.
  • Internet autopay: many providers discount autopay, but the size depends on how you pay. A typical structure is $10 a month off when autopay comes from a bank account, $5 from a debit card, and nothing from a credit card. At $50.48 a month all in for home internet in that example, the $10 bank account discount alone is worth $120 a year, forever, for a two minute settings change.
  • Phone: a prepaid phone plan can run to about $260 a year in total, with more monthly data than most people use, against postpaid plans that quietly cost two or three times that. The service difference for many people is close to zero. The price difference is not.

Add those three together and you are looking at something like $1,500 to $2,000 a year, every year, found by asking around and reading the settings page properly. That is an emergency fund, built out of phone calls. It is also why the Contribute section exists. Every person in this group knows one trick like this that the rest of us do not. Share yours. I will keep adding the good ones here, with credit.

Tax treatment of donations differs by country and changes over time. If you give larger amounts, check your country's official guidance, linked in the playbooks, on gift aid, deductions or credits before the tax year ends.

Do this todayDo the hour exercise once, write the number down, and set your giving as a named monthly line in your budget, at whatever amount is honest right now. Not the amount that sounds impressive. The amount you can sustain in a bad month too.

10 · Insurance

Insure what would break you. Carry the rest yourself.

Insurance is the least exciting section here and I will not pretend otherwise. It is also the one that protects every other section. One uninsured hospital stay, one accident with no cover, can erase a decade of good saving in a week. Insurance is not an investment and should never be sold to you as one. It is paying a small, known cost to remove a large, ruinous one. The test for any policy is simple: if this happened tomorrow with no insurance, would it wreck my finances for years? If yes, insure it. If it would just be annoying, save the premium and carry the risk yourself.

The checklist

  • Health: Non-negotiable where it is not provided by the state. Know what your employer or national system actually covers, not what you assume it covers. Those are often different lists.
  • Dental: Often separate from health cover. Compare a policy against simply saving the premium into a sinking fund, and check whether a discount or savings plan fits your situation better.
  • Auto: Legally required in most places. For an older, cheap car, consider whether full collision cover costs more than the car risk justifies.
  • Renters or contents: Cheap, and covers your belongings plus liability in someone else's building. Routinely skipped by renters who assume the landlord's policy covers them. It does not.
  • Life: Only when someone depends on your income: a partner, children, or family you support. If nobody does, you likely do not need it yet, whatever the salesperson's urgency says. When you do need it, simple term cover is usually the honest product.
  • Disability or income protection: Your income is your biggest asset in your twenties, it is not close. A policy that pays if you cannot work protects everything else on this site. Often overlooked, often available through employers, often never checked.
  • Travel: For trips abroad, especially where healthcare is expensive. Buy it for the medical cover, not the lost sunglasses.
Go deeperWhat you can probably skip for now, said carefully

I am not an insurance advisor, so treat this as how I think, not a ruling. In your twenties with no dependants, the policies that usually fail my test are the ones insuring small, annoying losses at large, certain cost: extended warranties, gadget cover, and whole life products sold as investments, which tend to be mediocre at both jobs. Meanwhile the ruinous risks, health, income, liability to others, are the ones people underbuy because they feel abstract. Flip that instinct. Expensive feelings, cheap facts: insure the catastrophe, sinking fund the annoyance, and write down what you hold so future you can check it in the monthly close.

Review cover when life changes: new job, new country, new dependant. And keep a simple record of what you hold, where, and when it renews, so a renewal price jump gets flagged in your monthly close instead of auto paying in silence. Quiet renewals at higher prices are exactly the pattern Section 2 teaches you to catch.

Do this todayList every policy you currently pay for and its renewal date. Almost everyone finds at least one they forgot they had, or one gap, usually contents or income protection, that actually matters.

Debt or invest? Put the two rates next to each other

The cleanest way I know to think about extra loan payments versus investing: take the same lump of money and ask what each route does to it over the same years. Paying off debt earns you its interest rate, guaranteed. Investing earns whatever the market gives, which is not guaranteed, and that difference in certainty is part of the answer, not a footnote.

Simplified on purpose: annual compounding, no tax, no fees, and it assumes the debt would otherwise sit there costing its rate, which is not exactly how amortising loans behave. Special cases change the answer: UK style student loans repaid through income, subsidised or 0% rates, and any employer match, which beats both and comes first. Use this to see the shape of the trade, then check your loan type in your country playbook.
