LEARN AT YOUR OWN PACE

The field guide

Open the decision you are facing. Each guide has a worked example, a next step, and sources you can check.

Read your pay and close the monthMake a plan on payday, then use the actual transactions to decide what you’ll change next month.

Read the deposit back to the payslip

Start with gross cash pay. Trace income tax withholding, social contributions and deductions for benefits or retirement until you get to the deposit. Some lines reduce taxable wages while others are taken after tax. Withholding is a prepayment and the final amount owed is worked out separately. Put payroll retirement savings in your savings total and leave them out of your take-home budget if they are already deducted.

Worked example

A synthetic US payslip shows $4,000 gross, $700 withheld for taxes, $150 for health insurance and $250 for retirement. The deposit is $2,900. A further $200 moved from the bank to savings brings the employee’s total saving to $450. A cash budget that starts at $2,900 only subtracts the $200 bank transfer.

Try this

Open your last payslip. Circle the amount that landed in your bank and label every line above it. Ask payroll about any line you cannot identify.

  • IRS withholding estimator FAQs

A big offer can have a smaller monthly paycheck

Write base pay, possible bonus and restricted stock units on separate lines. Base pay is the useful starting point for monthly commitments. A bonus can vary, and an equity grant has a vesting schedule and share-price risk. Even vested shares may have trading restrictions. In the US, equity compensation can create wage income and withholding. Review the grant, vest and tax records before treating sale proceeds as spendable cash.

Worked example

A hypothetical $250,000 offer includes $160,000 base salary, a $20,000 target bonus and $70,000 in annualized stock value at the grant price. Base gross pay is about $13,333 per month before deductions. If the share price halves before a scheduled vest and units are unchanged, that $70,000 illustration becomes $35,000 of stock value. Model fixed bills from base take-home pay, then decide how much employer stock you are comfortable holding given that your job already depends on the same company.

Try this

Find the first vest date, the vest schedule, how shares are withheld for taxes, and whether you can sell when you expect to.

  • IRS explanation of restricted stock and RSUs

One month in the Queens analyst’s budget

On payday, assign the deposit to essentials, debt, flexible spending, irregular bills and goals. At month-end, compare the plan with the transactions. Count purchases made on a credit card when they occur and record the card payoff as a transfer so the spending appears once. Record transfers to your own savings separately from spending. Check categories that keep going over and charges you do not recognize.

Worked example

A simplified month for the simulated Queens analyst starts with $5,900 take-home. Planned essentials of $3,150, minimum debt of $250, flexible spending of $850, sinking funds of $450 and goals of $1,200 use the full deposit. Essentials end up $70 over and flexible spending $160 over, including a $35 subscription renewal. Keeping the other transfers unchanged would leave a $230 shortfall. If the charge was legitimate and useful, budget it explicitly. If it was unwanted, cancel the next renewal and still account for what actually cleared this month.

Compare one month in the example Budget Plan and Money Tracker. Pick one recurring charge to confirm, one category to adjust, and one transfer to keep.

A bill you expect and a shock you can’t plan

A sinking fund is money set aside for a known bill, even if its amount is an estimate. An emergency reserve buys time when something uncertain happens. Size that reserve around obligations you still owe if income stops, including rent, minimum debt payments, and family support you intend to keep paying. Net worth answers a different question: assets minus liabilities. Assets you cannot access easily can increase net worth while leaving your emergency cover unchanged.

Worked example

$840 due in six months needs $140 per month if you have saved nothing. Separately, $3,600 of accessible cash against $1,800 of monthly essentials buys two months of cover. A retirement account can be part of net worth while the $3,600 remains the working emergency reserve.

Try this

Write the amount and due date for your next three irregular bills. Decide what you will pay from those funds and what would trigger use of the emergency reserve.

  • CFPB emergency fund guide
  • CFPB cash-flow budget worksheet

An unfamiliar charge deserves a closer look

Check merchant names, receipts, any authorized users, and subscription renewals. An unwanted renewal and unauthorized use need different responses. When you suspect fraud, contact the bank or card issuer promptly using the number on your card or the official app. Ask what steps and deadlines apply to your account. Save confirmation numbers and notices in one place.

Worked example

You find a $24 renewal and a $90 purchase from a merchant you cannot identify. Your email confirms the $24 renewal, so you can decide whether to cancel the next one. The $90 has no match in your records, so you contact the issuer and document the report. Keep the charge in your tracker while a dispute is pending and record any credit when it arrives.

Try this

Turn on transaction alerts where available. Keep the issuer’s verified contact details and check your statements regularly.

Choose a time horizon before you choose a tool

Give each goal an amount, date and spending currency. If the deadline is fixed, the contribution you can sustain and the downside you can afford matter more than an appealing rate. Split essentials from optional upgrades so you have somewhere to adjust if the plan is tight.

Worked example

A CAD 6,000 move in 18 months with CAD 2,400 already set aside needs CAD 200 per month before interest. If the date moves six months earlier, the same gap needs CAD 300. If CAD 200 is what fits, changing the moving budget or the date may be a stronger lever than reaching for return.

Try this

Write one goal due this year, one within five years and one further away. Give each its own monthly contribution and goal currency.

Protect the month you can’t affordCompare health plans, dental coverage and the risks that could undo your progress.

Compare health plans at three levels of use

Add 12 months of premiums to your estimated cost sharing. A deductible is what you pay for many covered services before the plan starts paying its share. A copay is a fixed amount, and coinsurance is a percentage of the allowed charge. The out-of-pocket maximum is a cap on qualifying cost sharing for covered in-network care, and premiums and uncovered care sit outside it. HMOs commonly restrict nonemergency care to their network. PPOs typically allow out-of-network care at additional cost. Check the actual network, referrals and covered drugs.

Worked example

Two hypothetical plans with your providers in network. Plan A costs $120 monthly with $200, $1,600 or $4,000 in cost sharing across three use levels, for totals of $1,640, $3,040 or $5,440. Plan B costs $225 monthly with $80, $700 or $2,500 in cost sharing, for totals of $2,780, $3,400 or $5,200. A costs less in the light and moderate examples, while B costs less in the heavy one. An excluded medication or provider could reverse the result.

Try this

Get the plan summaries and compare your two likely providers, recurring prescriptions, and your ability to cover a large bill early in the year.

  • HealthCare.gov guide to total plan costs
  • HealthCare.gov Summary of Benefits and Coverage
  • HealthCare.gov network and plan types

An HSA and a health FSA solve different problems

In the US, HSA contributions require qualifying coverage and eligibility under the rules for the year. HSA funds can generally carry forward and stay with you when you change jobs. A health FSA is an employer arrangement for eligible expenses with plan-year deadlines and possible carryover or a grace period. A general-purpose health FSA may affect HSA eligibility. Ask about limited-purpose arrangements if both appear in your benefits portal.

Worked example

You expect $400 of eligible expenses and are deciding whether to elect $400 or $1,000 in a health FSA. With no carryover or grace period and no additional eligible claims, the larger election risks forfeiting $600. If you also have an HSA option, confirm compatibility before making either election.

Try this

Ask benefits for the plan’s eligibility language, reimbursement deadline, carryover or grace-period rule, and any employer HSA contribution.

  • IRS Publication 969 on HSAs and FSAs

Follow one medical bill through the plan

Find the plan’s allowed amount and the deductible already satisfied. Some services use a copay or have special rules, so start with the applicable benefit. If the deductible applies, subtract the remaining deductible from the allowed charge before applying coinsurance to the rest. Then check what counts toward your out-of-pocket maximum.

Worked example

For a hypothetical in-network procedure with a $1,200 allowed charge, a $500 remaining deductible and 20% coinsurance, the patient pays $500 plus $140, or $640, assuming no cap has been reached and no other plan rules apply. With the deductible already satisfied, the same charge would cost $240 under those assumptions.

Try this

Ask the insurer for a written estimate using the procedure code, facility and clinician, including whether each bills separately.

  • HealthCare.gov guide to total plan costs
  • HealthCare.gov Summary of Benefits and Coverage

Run the dental numbers before you commit

Check waiting periods, what the plan classifies as major care, its allowed fee, participating dentists, annual benefit maximum and exclusions. A dental benefit maximum commonly caps what the insurer pays. A sinking fund can help cover predictable work or the share the plan leaves to you.

Worked example

A hypothetical $2,400 procedure is fully eligible at the allowed price. With a $50 deductible, 50% coverage after the deductible, a $1,000 plan benefit maximum and $360 annual premiums, the plan pays $1,000. You pay $1,760 including premiums. If a waiting period excludes the procedure, the same plan would leave $2,760 at that price. If paying $2,400 directly in 12 months, a $200 monthly sinking fund would cover that modeled bill.

Try this

Ask the dentist for procedure codes and a written cost estimate. Ask the insurer how it applies those codes, then compare with the self-pay quote and payment timing.

  • HealthCare.gov dental plan information

Audit the losses you could struggle to absorb

Start with a major liability claim, lost earnings, theft or loss at home, and financial obligations people depend on you to cover. Review auto liability limits and deductibles, renters replacement cost versus depreciated value, disability waiting periods and benefit definitions, term life needs if someone depends on your income, and travel medical or evacuation limits. Read exclusions and whether cover ends when a job or residency changes.

Worked example

Two hypothetical auto quotes differ by $600 a year. The cheaper one has a $1,500 collision deductible versus $500, and lower liability limits. If you could not find the extra $1,000 after a collision, the monthly saving comes with a real cash-flow trade-off. Get both quotes at the same limits and deductibles before comparing price.

Try this

Make a one-page policy list with premium, deductible, limit, exclusion and renewal date. Identify one large loss you could not cover yourself.

Rent, buy or shareCompare the cost of a place to live and the equity you may build, with room for the possibility that life moves you first.

Compare monthly costs, cash to close and exit equity

Rent buys shelter and flexibility. Ownership adds loan costs, property tax, insurance, repairs and sale costs, and payments can also build equity. Compare similar places, and use the same timeline and return assumptions on both sides. Make room for the renter’s ability to invest any up-front or monthly savings.

Worked example

A hypothetical $320,000 home with 10% down and a 30-year loan at 6.5% starts with about $1,820 of monthly principal and interest. Add $300 tax, $120 insurance, $250 maintenance reserve, $120 mortgage insurance and $110 association charges for about $2,720. At $2,100 rent with comparable utilities, renting frees about $620 monthly in this example. Ownership could build equity, and a sale within a few years could use much of that equity for transaction costs.

Try this

Put one actual rental and one comparable home listing side by side. Ask lenders for comparable Loan Estimates and get a local insurance estimate before modeling the exit.

  • CFPB guide to comparing Loan Estimates
  • CFPB guide to mortgage amortization

House hacking only works if you can carry the empty month

Separate rental income from the housing cost you still owe if a tenant leaves. Check owner-occupancy rules with the lender, legal permission to rent, insurance terms, tax treatment, and the work of being a landlord. Budget for vacancy, repair, turnover and collection. An insured owner-occupied loan requires genuine occupancy under its rules.

Worked example

At $500,000 with 5% down and a 30-year 6.5% mortgage, principal and interest are about $3,002 monthly. Two $1,000 rooms at a 10% vacancy allowance bring in $1,800 on average. A $5,000 annual maintenance reserve brings the remaining monthly cost to roughly $1,619, before tax, insurance, utilities, closing costs and other expenses. If both rooms are empty, the same subtotal rises to about $3,419. The incomplete subtotal is a resilience test.

Try this

Run a no-rent month and a major-repair year on the same sheet. Confirm occupancy and lease rules before treating any rent as part of the financing.

  • HUD FHA Single Family Housing Policy Handbook
  • CFPB guide to comparing Loan Estimates

Taking in a UK lodger

Taking a lodger in the home you occupy can reduce your own housing cost. Ask whether your mortgage or tenancy permits it and whether you need consent, whether insurance covers the arrangement, what legal duties apply, and how the furnished room income is treated. Tax relief and legal permission are separate checks.

Worked example

A room let for £550 per month for ten occupied months brings in £5,500. An extra £65 in bills for those months and a £300 turnover allowance reduce cash income to £4,550 before any applicable tax. If the lender, landlord or insurer withholds required permission, remove the income from your budget.

Try this

Read your mortgage or tenancy agreement and the official resident-landlord guide, then put permission and expected net proceeds on separate lines.

  • GOV.UK guide to letting rooms in your home
  • HMRC Rent a Room helpsheet

Verify Nigerian land before you pay

Have an independent local lawyer and surveyor verify the seller’s identity and authority, the chain of title, the land registry search, registered charges and disputes, the survey and boundaries, any government acquisition, and the consent and registration steps that apply. Ask what the seller’s document actually grants and who can legally transfer that interest. Use the relevant state or FCT authority rather than assuming one jurisdiction’s process applies everywhere.

Worked example

A hypothetical plot is quoted at ₦15 million. If your independent survey, consent and registration budget is ₦1.5 million and basic site work is ₦2 million, your working budget is ₦18.5 million before delays. If the documents and plot identifiers do not match, the next step is verification with the registry and surveyor before any deposit.

Try this

Ask your own lawyer for a written list of searches, their results, and the conditions to clear before releasing money.

House deposit or student loan?

List the loan type, rate, minimum payment, forgiveness or income-based terms, any prepayment costs, and the cash you need for a deposit and moving. An extra loan payment reduces future interest on some loans and is hard to reverse. Cash for a move keeps options open. A loan with forgiveness or income-based terms needs its own comparison.

Worked example

You have $4,000 in savings, a $3,000 cash floor and a $10,000 deposit goal. Sending $3,000 to the loan leaves $1,000 cash and breaks the floor. Keeping $3,000 as the reserve and splitting the other $1,000 keeps the move viable while you verify the loan terms.

Try this

Check the current rules with the loan servicer or official student aid office and ask how extra payments are applied.

Make a plan when work is changingBuild around the income you can count on while you job hunt, study, move, or test a second income.

Job hunting with a runway

Runway is accessible cash divided by the monthly amount you spend beyond dependable income. Budget for search expenses, deposits and the gap between accepting a job and receiving the first paycheck. If you are planning two timelines, use the slower one to decide which costs you can commit to now.

Worked example

With £4,000 cash, £1,150 of monthly essentials and £350 of reliable monthly income, you use £800 per month and have five months of runway. A £1,200 move leaves £2,800, or three and a half months at the same pace. If the move also changes your rent, rerun the model with the new monthly cost.

Try this

Give yourself a date to reassess housing and spending, then price out a slower job-search scenario.

First job benefits are part of the offer

Ask when health coverage starts, when you can enroll, what the employer contributes, when matching contributions vest, and whether there are fees or enrollment defaults. Review what happens if you leave early. Build a short cash reserve for the first month, when travel, a deposit or work equipment can arrive before pay.

Worked example

A hypothetical employer matches 50% of the first 6% contributed on $60,000 eligible pay. At a 6% employee contribution, you contribute $3,600 annually and the employer adds $1,800 before any vesting conditions. Contributing 3% would capture $900 under the same formula. Check the actual pay-period rules before making an annual assumption.

Try this

Ask HR for the written match formula, the vesting schedule and the health plan start date. Mark the dates on your first-month calendar.

Student work and tax residence are separate checks

Check whether your permission covers the exact work, hours, employer and start date. Remote freelancing and self-employment can have different treatment from payroll work. UK Student permission generally restricts self-employment. Irish student stamps have different conditions. Sweden changed higher-education permit rules in June 2026, with transitions and exceptions. In the US, F-1 practical training has its own authorization rules. Tax residence and payroll-tax treatment depend on separate tests.

Worked example

A student is offered eight hours of payroll work and four hours of remote contracting. Even if twelve hours fits a work-hours cap, the contract can be a separate permission question. Run your cash budget with the confirmed income only until the international office or immigration authority clarifies the rest.

Try this

Use your actual permission and course dates to ask the international office for written guidance. For a US paycheck, check tax residence and Social Security/Medicare treatment with the appropriate tax resources separately.

A side income has costs before it pays you

Price one small offer and track how many paid hours turn into unpaid admin, travel, sales and revisions. Take out direct expenses and reserve for tax under your local rules. Check any employer policy and your work authorization. Keep the money you need for rent and taxes outside the money you are prepared to risk on the idea.

Worked example

Four tutoring sessions at $50 produce $200. Materials and travel cost $28, leaving $172 before tax. Four teaching hours plus two hours of preparation mean about $28.67 per total hour before tax. If a nearby client removes one hour of travel or prep, the same $172 over five hours is $34.40. Compare that with other uses of the time.

Try this

Test one paid offer with a fixed cost and time limit. Keep receipts, record time and review whether you would do it again at that net rate.

Move countries with a cash plan first

Plan deposits, rent overlap, transportation, currency conversion and any gap in income or health cover. Keep track of where you live, where you are tax resident, which jurisdiction holds your investments, and the currency you will spend. Before you move, ask account providers what changes when your address or tax residence changes.

Worked example

You expect €1,400 for a deposit and €1,000 for first-month costs. Another €450 for transport, temporary accommodation and paperwork makes €2,850. A separate €600 buffer puts your working target at €3,450. Check the cash amount you actually receive after FX and fees when funding from another currency.

Spend deliberately, give deliberatelyDecide when paying for convenience is worth it and make room for the people and causes you care about.

What would you do with the time you buy back?

Divide the extra cost by the hours you genuinely get back. Then decide what those hours mean for you. Earning potential is one comparison, and rest, study and time with people count too. Look at the repeat cost if it becomes a habit and try a reversible version first.

Worked example

A service costs ₦24,000 more than doing the job yourself and frees three hours. That is ₦8,000 per hour. If it only saves an hour after coordination and travel, the price is ₦24,000 per hour. You might happily pay during a busy month and make a different decision once your schedule eases.

Try this

Try one purchase that saves time. Track what it actually cost and how you used the time before you repeat it.

Compare subscriptions and recurring bills over a full year

Put the total annual cost side by side. Check expiry dates, equipment, setup and early termination fees, and what each option actually provides. Before switching coverage, verify that the cheaper option still meets the need.

Worked example

A plan costs $60 monthly. An alternative costs $45 for six months and then $55, with a $40 setup fee. The annual comparison is $720 versus $640, a first-year saving of $80. If you would pay $100 to exit the old plan early, switching now costs $20 more over that period.

Try this

Pick one recurring bill. Request the full first-year and renewal costs in writing, then set a reminder before the next price change.

Make family support a line you can keep funding

Decide how much support can be recurring and what would count as an emergency request. Set the amount in the recipient’s currency when that is how bills are paid. Compare the amount delivered after fees and FX, the delivery time, and whether the recipient can access the funds.

Worked example

With a £200 total budget, one transfer costs £2 and converts the remaining £198 at an illustrative ₦1,900 per £1, delivering ₦376,200. Another charges no stated fee and uses ₦1,850 per £1, delivering ₦370,000. Compare recipient proceeds using quotes taken at the same time.

Give around the outcome you care about

Pick a goal for the gift before you choose an organization. Look for evidence of outcomes, what an additional contribution would fund, transparency and any values you want the giving to reflect. For tax purposes, verify eligibility independently in your jurisdiction. Track charitable donations and direct family support separately if their reporting differs.

Worked example

You budget $80 a month for giving. You might give $50 consistently to a community you know and reserve $30 for a program with published evidence you can review. If income drops, decide up front whether you prefer to pause both commitments or protect one and adjust the other.

Choose what to ownStart with what the money is for. Then compare cash, bonds, funds and stocks by the job each can do.

The account and the investment are separate choices

A 401(k), ISA or TFSA sets rules for taxes, contributions and access. Inside an eligible account, you choose what to hold. A cash deposit earns interest. A bond pays according to its terms and can change in price. A stock gives you a share of one business. A diversified fund can spread risk across many holdings, while still falling with the market. Property adds rent and resale exposure, plus costs and a potentially slow exit.

Worked example

Imagine £6,000 for a move next spring and £6,000 for a goal in 25 years. A 30% fall leaves either pot at £4,200. The first deadline gives you little time to recover. The second gives you more time, provided you can pay your bills without selling. That difference can change the asset choice even when both pots start at the same size.

Try this

Label each pot with amount, date, currency and the earliest date you might need it. Write down where it sits, what it holds and how quickly you could get it back.

  • SEC guide to asset allocation
  • Investor.gov investing basics

Read the fund before comparing the fee

An ETF trades on an exchange. An index fund tries to track a benchmark and can be an ETF or a mutual fund. Read the benchmark, geography, top holdings, fund domicile and distribution policy. Then check the expense ratio, tracking difference, bid-ask spread, platform charge, FX and applicable taxes. Owning several funds can still leave you holding much of the same thing.

Worked example

Two hypothetical funds track the same benchmark. An assumed 4% annual return on $100,000 becomes about $209,000 after 20 years at a flat 0.25 percentage-point fee drag, versus about $181,000 at a 1 percentage-point drag. This simple illustration subtracts the fee from each year's return and assumes no other costs or tax.

Try this

Open two prospectuses. Put the benchmark, top ten weight, annual fee and one-time trading cost side by side. Check whether the price difference buys you anything you actually want.

If you want to own an individual business

Start with how the company earns money. Read its annual report for revenue sources, risks, debt, cash flow and share count. Check recent filings for what changed. Then ask how much growth the price already assumes, what could break your thesis, and how much of your job, equity pay and portfolio depend on the same employer or industry.

Worked example

A fictional business earns $2 per share and trades at $40, or 20 times earnings. If earnings grow to $2.40 but investors later pay 15 times earnings, the price is $36. The company grew earnings 20% and the share price still fell 10%, before dividends and costs.

Try this

Write a three-line investment case: where the cash comes from, what would prove the case wrong, and how much you could lose without interrupting the plan. If you already hold the stock through work, count it in the same exposure.

Read your retirement benefitsFind the match, understand when it belongs to you, and compare the tax you save today with what you might pay later.

A match has a formula and may have a vesting clock

Ask the plan administrator for the match percentage, eligible pay, waiting period, vesting, per-pay-period calculation and whether there is a year-end true-up. Vesting tells you how much of the employer's contribution you can keep if you leave. Check how a contribution affects the cash you need for essentials and minimum payments.

Worked example

On $60,000 of eligible pay, a match of 50% on the first 6% gives $1,800 in employer contributions when you put in $3,600 across the year, assuming the plan counts all that pay and each contribution qualifies. If the example plan has 50% vesting when you leave, you keep $900 of that employer contribution before market gains or losses. Your own $3,600 remains yours.

Try this

Look for the plan's summary plan description and fee disclosure. Verify the match in your pay statement and the vested amount in the plan portal.

Traditional and Roth on the same pay budget

Traditional pre-tax 401(k) deferrals generally defer federal income tax until withdrawal. Roth 401(k) deferrals use after-tax pay and qualified withdrawals can be federal income-tax free. A Roth IRA is a separate account with its own contribution and income rules. Traditional IRA deductibility can also depend on income and workplace coverage.

Worked example

Start with $1,000 of gross pay, an assumed 22% federal tax on the last dollars earned, and 2× growth. A traditional contribution puts in $1,000 and reaches $2,000. After a hypothetical 15% tax on withdrawal, it leaves $1,700. Roth puts in the $780 left after today's tax and reaches $1,560 after a qualified withdrawal. At a 30% withdrawal tax, traditional instead leaves $1,400. This omits payroll and state tax, fees, and any employer match.

Try this

Compare equal reductions in today's take-home pay. Run more than one future tax assumption and check when the money can come out.

  • IRS IRA contributions and deductions

Backdoor Roth and the pro-rata calculation

The usual backdoor Roth discussion involves a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution followed by a Roth conversion. Federal reporting uses Form 8606. When you also hold pretax traditional, SEP or SIMPLE IRA money, the calculation considers the combined accounts, including the relevant year-end values and distributions. A workplace plan's after-tax conversion, often called mega backdoor Roth, is a different arrangement that depends on plan features and applicable limits.

Worked example

In a simplified case, you have $93,000 pretax and $7,000 of nondeductible basis in your traditional IRAs. If the only event is a $7,000 conversion, with no market movement and $93,000 left at year end, roughly 7% of the conversion is nontaxable. About $490 is basis and about $6,510 is generally taxable. The account you convert from does not isolate the basis.

Try this

List every traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRA and locate prior Forms 8606 before acting. Confirm plan features, eligibility and reporting with the administrator and a qualified tax professional if your situation is complex.

Treasury bills, bonds, savings bonds and funds

A bill typically pays face value at maturity and is commonly bought below that amount. A fixed-rate bond pays coupons and returns principal according to its terms. If you sell a marketable bond early, its price can change when rates move. Bond funds hold a portfolio and can fluctuate with rates and credit conditions. In the U.S., marketable TIPS adjust principal with the specified inflation index, while Series I savings bonds have different accrual and redemption rules.

Worked example

A fictional 90-day bill costs $970 and pays $1,000. The $30 gain is 3.09% of your $970 outlay over the holding period before fees and tax. A discount quote expressed against face value starts from $30 divided by $1,000 and may annualize on a different day count. Neither figure tells you the rate at the next auction.

Try this

Write down amount paid, amount received, dates, fees and the source's exact rate label. Confirm the day count before using a quoted annual yield to infer the price.

A government bill, an international USD sovereign bond and a dollar mutual fund can have different issuers, fee layers, access and exit routes.

Worked example

CBN's October 28, 2026 Nigerian auction table shows a 91-day bill and a 15.40% Issue rate. That dated quote is not a 15.40% gain in 91 days or an executable quote today. For an unrelated fictional Ghana bill, if GH₵960 paid today buys GH₵1,000 at maturity, the period gain is GH₵40 ÷ GH₵960, or 4.17%, before fees and taxes. Use the issue date and rate label when reading a real auction.

Options, short selling, margin and stop orders

A call gives its buyer the right to buy at the strike, and a put gives the right to sell. Options expire and contract size matters. A long option can lose its entire premium. An uncovered short call can generate losses beyond the premium it earned. Shorting stock can have theoretically unlimited loss, and margin can leave you owing money after a forced sale. A stop order triggers a market order and its execution price can move past the stop. A stop-limit order can remain unfilled.

Worked example

One fictional call with a $50 strike, a $2 premium and a 100-share multiplier costs $200. At expiry, a $48 share price produces a $200 loss. At $53, the $300 intrinsic value leaves $100 of profit before costs. A share price of $52 breaks even at expiry. That expiry math does not describe the option's price before expiry.

Try this

Before evaluating any leveraged trade, write down contract size, expiry, cash at risk, assignment or liquidation mechanics, and what a gap in price could do.

A friend's deal still needs an independent check

Get the issuer's legal name, what you own, how returns are generated, existing senior debt, and who holds client money. Ask how the business can pay in a bad year, what makes any collateral enforceable, whether you can exit, and who is paid to bring you in. For a pool or club, establish who votes, values the assets, signs transfers and takes over if someone leaves. Registration and offering obligations depend on jurisdiction and facts.

Worked example

Four friends put $2,500 each into an asset. If it sells for $8,000 and $3,000 of senior costs and obligations are paid first, $5,000 remains, or $1,250 each under an equal split. Each lost half before tax. If proceeds do not cover senior obligations, equity can go to zero. A separate personal guarantee could create further liability.

Try this

Ask for contracts and financial statements, then verify claims with the relevant regulator or a qualified professional outside the deal. Pause when the cash flow, repayment priority or legal rights are unclear. A research-only club can share the work while each person keeps control of their own money.

With crypto, separate the asset from who can move it

Self-custody gives you responsibility for keys and backups. With a platform, check who controls the keys, how customer assets are held, whether the platform can lend them out and what rights you have if it fails. A stablecoin adds a separate question about redemption and reserves. A proof-of-reserves report can show some assets at a point in time and still leave questions about liabilities, ownership and what customers could recover.

Worked example

Suppose a fictional $1,000 token holding falls to $600 while the platform pauses withdrawals. The statement displays $600 and you still cannot use it for an urgent bill. A self-custodied token that falls by the same amount can have a different access problem if the keys are lost. In both cases, mark separately what the holding is worth and when you can actually use it.

Try this

Read the custody and withdrawal terms. Check who can move the funds, what happens in bankruptcy and which recovery options exist. Keep cash needed for a fixed date separate from this risk.

Check the person, the product and the payment instructions

Find the firm using the regulator's own register and compare its exact legal name, permission, contact details and public disciplinary record. Ask whether the person is acting as a broker or investment adviser, who pays them and what the total costs are. Treat pressure to pay, a screenshot of returns, a copied registration number and instructions to send funds to a personal account as reasons to pause.

Worked example

A message says a firm can pay 3% every month with no risk. Monthly compounding at that rate would imply about 42.6% a year before fees. That arithmetic is a reason to ask how the obligation is funded and enforced, and why the issuer needs this financing. It tells you nothing about whether the return can actually be paid.

Try this

Contact the firm using the number from the official register, then check whether the specific service and payment instructions match. For professional advice, request written fees, conflicts, scope and custody arrangements.
